Lobster Loader

Designing a 3D combo puzzle that was easy to read, satisfying to manipulate and deep enough to reward players who wanted to optimize.

01 · Production strategy

Choosing polish over scope

During concept selection, I advocated for the simplest of our four ideas: a 3D package-stacking puzzle about a tiny loader filling whales. The reasoning was production-focused. A smaller core system would give the team more time for responsiveness, audiovisual feedback, presentation and iteration before VFS Pitch & Play.

The goal was not “make the biggest game.” It was “make a small game feel exceptionally finished.”

02 · Design pillars

Three rules for every decision

Forward Motion

Progress should not be stopped or reset during a level. Mechanics should push the player more or less, rather than erase momentum.

Small Number Gets Bigger

Every package contributes to the quota. Good decisions should cause points to accelerate and make the player feel the number rapidly growing.

Flexibility

Players should have more than one viable choice. Constraints should be few, intuitive and leave room for personal strategy.

03 · Spatial readability

Rotate the puzzle, not the camera

The earliest composition placed the whale to one side of the screen. I pushed to center the whale and grid because that was where the player made every important spatial decision. Centering it improved hierarchy but created a new 3D problem: foreground packages could hide usable space behind them.

We prototyped player-controlled camera rotation, but it did not feel right. The better solution was to keep the camera stable and let the whale’s tongue manipulate the grid itself. Players could rotate the grid and tip it toward the camera to inspect different angles without losing the central composition.

Occlusion was not just visual. If the camera made a valid space impossible to inspect, the interface was effectively punishing the player.

Some older testers still felt the grid was too distant, so we added an optional zoom mode. Adoption was limited, so today I would explore a more integrated accessibility solution.

04 · Interaction design

Reducing cognitive load

Early builds exposed full 3-axis package rotation with six inputs. Testing showed that players rarely needed all of them, but still had to remember which key controlled which axis.

We simplified rotation to four inputs: W/S for forward/backward rotation and A/D for clockwise/counter-clockwise rotation. Players could still reach the necessary orientations without thinking in terms of pitch, yaw and roll.

Teaching was not the same as remembering

The forced tutorial itself tested reasonably well. The problem appeared in later levels, when players forgot that grid rotation or package rotation existed. We added a persistent question-mark control reference and worked with programmers on contextual prompts that resurfaced an input when the player had not used an important action for a while.

The onboarding problem was not “Can the player follow the tutorial?” It was “Can the player recall the tool when the tutorial is gone?”
05 · Game feel

Making the score feel physical

We wanted every meaningful placement to reinforce the player through multiple channels. Packages wiggle, particles fire, placement and scoring use distinct sounds, and generated point values appear above the source package before traveling toward the quota.

When a multiplier affects a package multiple times, the package reacts repeatedly and emits repeated score values. That makes an abstract scoring relationship easier to read: package → reaction → points generated → points move to the total.

More feedback and more sound were recurring requests in earlier playtests. We kept layering feedback until the feel of the game became one of the most consistent strengths in later testing.

06 · Emergent behavior

Players kept playing it “wrong”

Our strategic reference was Balatro: we wanted players to chase combinations, multipliers and the satisfying aha moment of finding a high-value interaction. In testing, many players instead approached the game like Tetris and focused on filling the spatial grid cleanly.

At first we treated that as a problem because those players tended to score less. Then we changed the design question: instead of forcing the intended strategy, how could we reward the behavior players already found fun without flattening the deeper scoring system?

The solution was a Layer Completion Bonus: fill a complete layer and receive the base value of every package in that layer again.

That gave spatial players a meaningful reward while letting optimization-focused players incorporate the same bonus into advanced multiplier strategies. One mechanic supported both playstyles and reinforced all three design pillars.

07 · Evidence

Five milestones, 103 responses

We combined daily internal testing, two consistent external testers who followed builds over time, fresh-eyes sessions roughly every two weeks and formal milestone forms across M1, M2, M3, Alpha and Beta/Pitch & Play.

103submitted playtest responses across five milestone forms
17/17Beta respondents said the game felt satisfying to play

The forms also show the direction of the design problem. Early testers strongly defaulted to filling the space; later Beta responses were more likely to cite raising the score as their instinctive goal. Because tester pools and questions changed, I treat this as directional evidence rather than a controlled experiment.

08 · Outcome

Polish became the result

Pitch & Play went extremely well. Visitors consistently praised the game’s feel, visual quality, immersion and level of polish, and people generally understood the concept quickly. The showcase build included three levels, with three more added shortly afterward.

What I am proudest of is that the final reception matched the production decision we made at the beginning: protect scope so the team has time to polish. Six months is not long for a game production, but we stayed focused enough to make the core interaction feel complete.

What I would improve with one more month

I would make onboarding more subtle and integrated, revisit the quota/HUD composition, expand package modifiers and constraints, and build more levels around intentional grid variations instead of mainly increasing numerical difficulty. The grid system was flexible enough to support much more design depth.

Production support beyond UX

Alongside PM, game design and UX/UI responsibilities, I modeled and UV-unwrapped a set of package props when the project needed additional 3D production support.
