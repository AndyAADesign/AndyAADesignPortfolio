Lobster Loader

01 · Production strategy

Choosing polish over scope

We had four concepts. Lobster Loader was the smallest, and that was exactly why I pushed for it. Our instructors kept emphasizing that a polished game would land better at Pitch & Play than a bigger idea we barely finished. I wanted enough time to tune controls, feedback, readability and presentation instead of spending the whole production just trying to get the core game working.

Make a small game feel exceptionally finished.

02 · Design pillars

Three rules for every decision

01

Forward Motion

Keep the player moving. Slow them down if needed, but don’t erase their progress.

02

Small Number Gets Bigger

Every package should move the score. Good combinations should make that number jump.

03

Flexibility

Give players several good choices instead of one correct answer.

03 · Spatial readability

Rotate the puzzle, not the camera

The whale and grid originally sat off to the side. I wanted the actual decision space in the center, where the player was already looking. That immediately improved the hierarchy, but it created a new problem: packages in front could hide valid spaces behind them.

ProblemThe centered grid was easier to read overall, but front pieces could block useful information.
PrototypeWe tried player-controlled camera rotation. It worked technically, but it didn’t feel right in playtests.
SolutionKeep the camera stable and let the whale’s tongue rotate and tilt the grid instead.

Some testers still felt the grid was too far away, so we added an optional zoom. It helped, but it was more of a practical accommodation than a complete accessibility solution. I’d handle that more elegantly now.

04 · Interaction design

Six rotation keys were too much

ObservedPlayers didn’t need all six rotation inputs often enough to remember which key controlled each axis.
ChangeW/S became forward/backward rotation; A/D handled clockwise/counter-clockwise.
Follow-upThe tutorial worked, but players still forgot tools later. Contextual prompts brought those actions back after long periods of non-use.
Teaching the controls once wasn’t the same problem as helping people remember them ten minutes later.
05 · Game feel

Making the score feel physical

Every successful placement needed to say, “yes, that mattered.” So packages wiggle, particles fire, placement and scoring use different sounds, and point values appear at the source before flying toward the quota. Multipliers repeat those reactions so players can actually see where the value came from.

“More feedback” and “more sound” kept showing up in early tests. We kept adding reinforcement until game feel became one of the things people praised most consistently.

06 · Emergent behavior

Players kept playing it “wrong”

We wanted Balatro-style scoring: notice synergies, build multipliers and chase a bigger number. But a lot of people played it like Tetris instead, trying to fill the space neatly. At first that was frustrating. Then we realized the better answer was to reward the behavior they already enjoyed.

BehaviorSome players cared more about filling layers cleanly than squeezing every point out of the scoring system.
Design responseCompleting a layer awards the base value of every package in that layer again.
Why it workedTetris-like players got a satisfying payoff, while optimizers could still stack the same bonus with multipliers.
One mechanic supported both playstyles instead of forcing everyone into the same “correct” strategy.
07 · Evidence

Five milestones, 103 submitted responses

Across M1, M2, M3, Alpha and Beta/Pitch & Play, our forms collected 103 submitted responses, not 103 unique people. We also did daily internal checks, kept two recurring external testers for continuity and brought in fresh eyes roughly every two weeks.

08 · Outcome

Polish was the whole point

At Pitch & Play, people kept praising the exact things we had protected time for: game feel, visual quality, immersion and polish. The concept also read quickly, which mattered for a showcase where people were jumping between games. The event build had three levels; we added three more shortly afterward.

Proudest ofWe picked polish as the production goal on day one, and that’s what people noticed at the end.
One more monthI’d make onboarding more subtle, revisit the HUD composition and push deeper on package modifiers, constraints and levels built around specific grid shapes.

Production support beyond UX

When the art side needed extra help, I also modeled and UV-unwrapped package props for the final game.
