Xapontic

A real-client e-commerce project where we had to make buying soap simple without stripping away the story of the women and community behind it.

01 · Context

The challenge

Xapontic makes artisanal soaps through the work of Tseltal women in northern Chiapas. They didn’t have a website when we started. Our brief was to create the customer-facing site and a backend the team could update without touching code.

The UX question was simple: how do we tell the story behind the product without making it harder to browse and order?

I owned most of the front-end experience: wireframes, interactive prototypes, visual UI, responsive layouts, usability testing and HTML/CSS/JavaScript. Another teammate led most of the backend and database work.

02 · Information architecture

Start broad, then let people go deeper

We started with card sorting around the content the client actually needed: story, community, products, events, news, contact and ordering. That gave us a main navigation of Inicio → Nosotras → Productos → Noticias → Contacto, with Realizar pedido kept separate as the main commerce action.

The homepage gives you the map. Inner pages let you dig in.

We put the story before the catalog on purpose. For Xapontic, who made the product and why mattered to the value of the product itself. The homepage gave people a quick overview, then let them jump into products, bazaars, partners or contact information.

03 · Commerce flow

Two ways to start the same order

Some people already knew they wanted to order. Others wanted to look around first. So we kept a clear order CTA available throughout the site, while still letting people browse the catalog and enter the same flow from there.

ProblemRequired choices and optional customization were competing for attention.
DecisionBreak the flow into product selection, optional customization, customer details, review and confirmation.
ResultThe main task stayed simple, while customization was still there for people who wanted it.
04 · Usability testing

Could people actually find what they needed?

At several milestones, we ran task-based usability tests with roughly 15 to 20 participants per round. We asked people to find products, events, company information and ingredients, contact the brand and complete an order.

Most participants were university students or people reasonably close to the intended buyer, so I treat the findings as directional rather than rigorous market research.

EvidencePeople had to complete real tasks, not just tell us whether they liked the screens.
Observed frictionSoap sizes depended too much on gram weights, which made relative size differences harder to scan.
TodayI’d keep the exact weights, but add much stronger visual cues for comparing sizes.
05 · Responsive design

Desktop-down got messy. So we flipped it.

We first tried designing large desktop layouts and shrinking them down. It got crowded fast. So we switched to Mobile → Tablet → Desktop and forced ourselves to decide what mattered on the smallest screen first.

ConstraintDesktop-first layouts became crowded and awkward when we tried to squeeze them onto mobile.
ChangeDesign Mobile → Tablet → Desktop instead of shrinking from the largest canvas.
BenefitThe hierarchy got clearer, responsive behavior was easier to plan and the front-end became more practical to build.
06 · Outcome & reflection

What shipped and what I learned

The front end worked and we presented it as a functional responsive site. The backend/CMS never became as stable as we wanted, and another student team was selected by the client. That was a useful production lesson: a strong interface can’t carry a product if the rest of the system isn’t equally solid.

But the project still earned a Mención Honorífica at Diseña México 2020 in the student website category. The jury highlighted the client and user needs behind the design, the user evaluation and the way the site represented the community, production process and ordering experience.

What I’d change today

I’d make the buttons feel more obviously clickable, clean up the product hierarchy and make size comparison much more visual. I’d also push the interaction design further before letting implementation anxiety narrow the options. And on the production side, I’d identify technical risks earlier and make sure critical knowledge isn’t sitting with one person.
